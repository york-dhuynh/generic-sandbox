# What kinds of repetition help: the evidence

Research done 2026-10-28 to check the daily rotation (`scenarios/README.md`) against the learning-science literature. The sources are peer-reviewed papers, mostly meta-analyses, each linked by DOI.
- **Verified against the paper's own text:** Cepeda et al. (2008).
- **Verified from abstracts or index pages:** the other sources.
- **Unverified:** the numbers listed at the end.

## The short version

1. **Repetition helps when it's retrieval, spaced out, with feedback.** Rereading and same-sitting repetition do little.
2. **Get the gap long enough.** For a test about 1–2 months out, the best gap between sessions is roughly 1–2 weeks. Too short a gap costs much more than too long a gap.
3. **Retrieve each item about 3 times, in separate sessions.** Don't retire an item after one success, and don't trust your own sense that you "know it".
4. **Mix related material in review (interleaving).** It helps most when the options look alike but differ, which is exactly what "most effective fix" items test.
5. **Practise in the exam's format before the exam,** and anchor the schedule to the exam date.

## Kinds of repetition

| Kind | What it is | Evidence | Size | Practical setting | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Spacing | The same material again in separate sessions | Very strong | Best gap vs. no gap: +59% recall at a 35-day test delay (+64% overall) | Best gap ≈ 23% of the test delay at 35 days (~8 days) and 17% at 70 days (~12 days). "Costs of using too short a gap" are "much" larger than those of a gap that is too long | [Cepeda 2008](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02209.x), [Cepeda 2006](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.132.3.354) |
| Retrieval practice | Answering from memory instead of rereading | Very strong, in labs and classrooms | g ≈ 0.50. Larger with feedback (0.73 vs 0.39 in the lab), and grows with more quizzes (0.44 → 0.64) | Always give feedback. About 3 or more retrievals per item | [Rowland 2014](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0037559), [Yang 2021](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000309) |
| Spaced retrieval | Retrieval attempts on different days | Strong | g = 0.74 vs. retrieval in one sitting | Repeats within one sitting don't count as spacing | [Latimier 2021](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-020-09572-8) |
| Successive relearning | Retrieve until correct, then relearn in later sessions | Strong, including real course exams | Large | 3 correct recalls in the first session, then about 3 relearning sessions at widely spaced intervals | [Rawson & Dunlosky 2011](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0023956), [review 2013](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-013-9240-4) |
| Expanding vs. equal gaps | Gaps that grow vs. gaps that stay the same | Consistent: little or no difference | g ≈ 0.03 | Delay the first spaced review. The shape of the schedule barely matters | [Karpicke & Roediger 2007](https://doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.33.4.704), [Kang 2014](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-014-0636-z) |
| Interleaving | Mixing categories during practice | Moderate, depends on the material | g = 0.42 overall. Helps with similar-looking categories; can hurt with word lists | Best when items look alike but differ | [Brunmair & Richter 2019](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000209) |
| Feedback | The answer, or an explanation, after responding | Strong | d ≈ 0.48. Explanations beat the bare answer for new inference questions | Multiple choice without feedback can teach you the wrong answer you picked | [Butler et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0031026), [Butler & Roediger 2008](https://doi.org/10.3758/MC.36.3.604) |
| Pretesting | Trying questions before studying | Moderate, growing | Beat extra study time, even on items answered wrong | The answers must be studied afterwards. Multiple-choice pretests also help related material | [Richland et al. 2009](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0016496), [Pan & Carpenter 2023](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-023-09814-5) |
| Hypercorrection | High-confidence errors are corrected more readily after feedback | Established | – | Corrected high-confidence errors can come back, so retest them after at least a week | [Butterfield & Metcalfe 2001](https://doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.27.6.1491), [Butler et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-011-0173-y) |
| Transfer practice | Applying the same rule in new situations | Moderate | d ≈ 0.40, often smaller after correcting for bias | Use application and inference questions, not repeated identical prompts | [Pan & Rickard 2018](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000151) |
| Self-explanation | Explaining why something is true | Moderate | g = 0.55 | Prompt for "why" | [Bisra et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-018-9434-x), [Dunlosky et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100612453266) |
| Overlearning | Drilling past mastery in one sitting | Weak for the long term | Big at 1 week, "decreased dramatically" by 9 weeks | Skip it. Spend the time on spacing | [Rohrer et al. 2005](https://doi.org/10.1002/acp.1083) |
| Sleep | Consolidation between sessions | Moderate | g = 0.44 (0.28 after correcting for publication bias) | Have at least one night between sessions on the same material | [Berres & Erdfelder 2021](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000350), [Mazza et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797616659930) |
| Dropping items | Retiring items after you get them right | Consistent harm | Dropping after one recall cost a lot | Keep retrieving. Grade against a written answer, not your feeling | [Karpicke & Roediger 2008](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1152408) |
| Rereading | Reading the material again | Rated low utility | Not significant | Use it only as a comparison, never as review | [Callender & McDaniel 2009](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cedpsych.2008.07.001) |

## How the rotation measures up

**What it already does right:**
- retrieval with confidence ratings
- feedback after the block, not during it
- explanation-style work-backs (self-explanation)
- transfer questions in new situations
- interleaved review across scenarios
- a cold block the day after an overview: the block acts as a pretest

**Where it's weak, and the evidence-based fix:**

1. **The review ladder is too short.**
   - The same-day review is retrieval in one sitting, not spacing. That leaves only +1 and +7 as spaced reviews, and a rule retired on day 8 may go untouched for 3–7 weeks before the exam.
   - **Fix:** count the same-day review as initial learning. Then review at **+2, +7, +14**, move the rule to a maintenance pool that's sampled weekly, and retrieve everything in a final sweep.
   - **After a miss:** relearn it that day, then re-enter at +2 instead of starting over.
   - Sources: Latimier, Rawson & Dunlosky, Karpicke & Roediger 2008, Cepeda.
2. **The per-scenario cap backs up the scenario you just deep-dived.**
   - A block produces 4–7 rules, but only 3 get reviewed that day. They pile up behind that scenario's cap. Other scenarios aren't affected, because the cap is per scenario.
   - **Fix:** use one queue ordered by due date with a total time cap (about 15–20 minutes). Do same-day reviews in the last minutes of the deep dive, outside the cap.
3. **Every review uses the same free-recall prompt.**
   - Free recall is good: it's self-explanation, and in classrooms it ties with multiple choice. But it never practises rejecting plausible wrong options, which is what this exam tests.
   - A fixed prompt can also turn into memorizing a cue and its answer.
   - **Fix:** write a new situation at each step. Make the +7 step a fresh multiple-choice item with near-miss wrong options and explanation feedback.
   - Sources: Yang 2021, Rowland 2014, Pan & Rickard 2018.
4. **Pre-questions are guessed, not committed to.**
   - **Fix:** in the overview, commit to an answer and a confidence rating for each pre-question, then settle each one explicitly the next day. This sets up hypercorrection and makes the pretest effect real.
   - Sources: Richland 2009, Butterfield & Metcalfe 2001.
5. **Nothing is anchored to the exam date.**
   - **Fix:** set a date, or plan for 8 weeks, since overshooting is cheaper than undershooting.
     - Last 2–3 weeks: 2–3 full timed mocks, with scenarios presented in blocks as on the exam.
     - Last 7–10 days: a cumulative sweep of every rule, retired ones included, ending 2–3 days before the exam.
     - Retest corrected high-confidence errors at least a week later.
   - Sources: Cepeda 2008, Yang 2021 (practice that matches the exam format: g 0.53 vs 0.40), Butler et al. 2011.
6. **Doing two rotation days in one calendar day** (`cca.py day --advance`) skips a night of sleep between sessions. Avoid it except to catch up on a missed overview.
   - Sources: Mazza 2016, Berres & Erdfelder 2021.

## Not verified

The research agent couldn't confirm these figures from primary text. Don't quote them as fact:
- Adesope et al. (2017): its overall g and its multiple-choice vs. short-answer split
- Rawson, Dunlosky & Sciartelli (2013): the "about a letter grade" and "40% higher" figures
- Pan & Carpenter (2023): whether the prequestion benefit survives a 24-hour gap, and whether it covers material that wasn't prequestioned
- Karpicke & Roediger (2008): the often-quoted 80% vs. 35% (its abstract says only "large")
- Dunlosky & Rawson (2012): how learners come to drop items too early

No study tested interleaving with scenario-based judgment items directly. Applying it here is an extrapolation from category learning.
